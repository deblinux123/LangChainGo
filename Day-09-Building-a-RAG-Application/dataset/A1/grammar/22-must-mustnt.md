# Chapter 22 — Modal Verb: Must and Mustn't

## 1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Use **must** to express strong obligation and necessity.
- Use **mustn't** to express prohibition.
- Make positive and negative sentences with must.
- Ask questions using must.
- Understand the difference between **mustn't** and **don't have to**.
- Choose between **should**, **must**, and **have to** in simple situations.

## 2. What Does Must Mean?

We use **must** to talk about something that is very important or necessary.

It can express:

- Strong obligation
- Rules
- Important responsibilities
- Necessity

Examples:

- You must wear a seat belt.
- Students must do their homework.
- I must finish this project today.
- We must be quiet in the library.
- You must show your ticket.

**Structure:**

Subject + must + base form of the verb

| Subject | Modal | Verb | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | must | study | I must study. |
| You | must | listen | You must listen. |
| He | must | work | He must work. |
| She | must | leave | She must leave. |
| We | must | wait | We must wait. |
| They | must | follow | They must follow the rules. |

Important: We use the base form of the verb after **must**.

- She must go. (Correct)
- She must goes. (Incorrect)
- She must to go. (Incorrect)

## 3. Must for Rules and Obligations

We often use must when talking about rules at school, work, public places, or home.

### At School

- Students must arrive on time.
- You must bring your textbook.
- Students must respect their teachers.
- You must complete your homework.

### At Work

- Employees must wear their ID cards.
- We must attend the meeting.
- You must follow the safety rules.
- Everyone must arrive before 9:00 a.m.

### In Public Places

- You must wait in line.
- Passengers must show their tickets.
- Drivers must stop at a red light.
- You must keep this area clean.

### At Home

- I must clean my room.
- We must help our parents.
- You must turn off the lights.
- He must feed the dog.

## 4. Negative Form: Mustn't

**Mustn't** is the contraction of **must not**.

We use mustn't to say that something is prohibited or not allowed.

**Structure:**

Subject + mustn't + base form of the verb

Examples:

- You mustn't smoke here.
- Students mustn't use phones during the exam.
- You mustn't park in front of this door.
- We mustn't make noise in the library.
- Children mustn't play with fire.
- You mustn't touch that machine.

### Mustn't for Rules

| Situation | Example |
|---|---|
| Library | You mustn't talk loudly. |
| Hospital | You mustn't smoke here. |
| School | Students mustn't cheat. |
| Road | You mustn't cross the street here. |
| Workplace | You mustn't enter this room. |

## 5. Must vs. Mustn't

These two forms have opposite meanings.

| Must | Mustn't |
|---|---|
| It is necessary. | It is prohibited. |
| You must wear a helmet. | You mustn't ride without a helmet. |
| You must stop here. | You mustn't park here. |
| You must listen carefully. | You mustn't make noise. |

Compare:

- You must wear a seat belt. (It is necessary.)
- You mustn't drive without a seat belt. (It is prohibited.)

## 6. Questions with Must

We can put **must** before the subject to make a question.

**Structure:**

Must + subject + base form of the verb + ?

Examples:

- Must I finish this today?
- Must we wait here?
- Must she attend the meeting?
- Must they wear uniforms?
- Must he bring his passport?

### Short Answers

| Question | Short Answer |
|---|---|
| Must I leave now? | Yes, you must. |
| Must we wait? | No, you don't have to. |
| Must she come today? | Yes, she must. |
| Must they wear uniforms? | No, they don't have to. |

Important: For a negative short answer, **No, you don't have to** is commonly used to say that something is not necessary.

## 7. Wh-Questions with Must

We can use question words before must.

Examples:

- What must I bring?
- Where must we wait?
- When must she arrive?
- Why must I wear this uniform?
- How must we complete this form?

The structure is:

Question word + must + subject + base verb + ?

## 8. Mustn't vs. Don't Have To

This is an important difference.

**Mustn't** means something is prohibited. You cannot do it.

**Don't have to** means something is not necessary. You can do it, but it is optional.

| Mustn't | Don't Have To |
|---|---|
| It is not allowed. | It is not necessary. |
| You mustn't park here. | You don't have to pay today. |
| You mustn't use your phone. | You don't have to bring your laptop. |
| You mustn't enter this room. | You don't have to come early. |

Compare:

- You mustn't use your phone during the exam. (It is prohibited.)
- You don't have to bring your phone to class. (It is not necessary.)

Remember:

- **Mustn't = Don't do it.**
- **Don't have to = You don't need to do it.**

## 9. Should vs. Must

Both words are modal verbs, but they have different levels of strength.

**Should** gives advice or a recommendation.

**Must** expresses a strong obligation or an important rule.

| Should | Must |
|---|---|
| You should exercise. | You must wear a seat belt. |
| You should drink more water. | You must stop at a red light. |
| You should study more. | You must follow the exam rules. |

Examples:

- You should sleep earlier. (This is good advice.)
- You must be at the airport on time. (This is an important requirement.)

## 10. Must vs. Have To

At this level, both **must** and **have to** can express obligation.

Examples:

- I must finish my homework.
- I have to finish my homework.
- She must wear a uniform.
- She has to wear a uniform.

Remember the basic forms of have to:

| Subject | Form |
|---|---|
| I / You / We / They | have to |
| He / She / It | has to |

Examples:

- I have to work today.
- She has to study English.
- They have to arrive early.

The negative forms are different:

- You mustn't smoke here. (Smoking is prohibited.)
- You don't have to work tomorrow. (Working tomorrow is not necessary.)
- She doesn't have to come early. (Coming early is not necessary.)

## 11. Common Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Adding -s after the verb

Incorrect: He must works.

Correct: He must work.

### Mistake 2: Using to after must

Incorrect: I must to study.

Correct: I must study.

### Mistake 3: Using do in a must question

Incorrect: Do you must leave?

Correct: Must you leave?

In everyday American English, people also commonly ask:

- Do you have to leave?

### Mistake 4: Confusing mustn't and don't have to

Incorrect meaning: You mustn't come early. (when you mean it is optional)

Correct: You don't have to come early.

### Mistake 5: Using mustn't with a past verb

Incorrect: You mustn't went there.

Correct: You mustn't go there.

## 12. American English Notes

- **Mustn't** is grammatically correct in American English, but it is less common in everyday conversation than **can't** or **not allowed to** when expressing prohibition.
- Americans often say: "You can't park here."
- They also say: "You're not allowed to enter."
- **Must** is common in signs, official rules, instructions, and formal situations.
- **Have to** is very common in everyday American conversation.

Examples:

- You must show your ID. (Formal instruction.)
- You have to show your ID. (Common conversation.)
- You can't smoke here. (Everyday prohibition.)
- You mustn't smoke here. (Strong rule.)

## 15. Chapter Summary

In this chapter, you learned:

- **Must** expresses strong obligation, necessity, and rules.
- **Mustn't** expresses prohibition.
- Use the base form of the verb after must and mustn't.
- Questions with must begin with the modal verb.
- **Mustn't** means something is prohibited.
- **Don't have to** means something is not necessary.
- **Should** gives advice, while **must** expresses stronger obligation.
- **Have to** is also common for obligations in everyday American English.

### Quick Review

| Expression | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Must | Strong obligation | You must study. |
| Mustn't | Prohibition | You mustn't smoke here. |
| Don't have to | No necessity | You don't have to come. |
| Should | Advice | You should rest. |
